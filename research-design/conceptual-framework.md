# Nature-to-Finance Materiality: Conceptual Framework

> **From ecological exposure to informational visibility and financial decision-making**

## 1. Scientific Motivation

Biodiversity loss is increasingly recognised as a potential source of economic and
financial risk. Firms depend on ecosystem services while simultaneously generating
impacts on ecosystems through their operations, locations, supply chains and production
processes.

However, ecological relevance does not automatically imply financial relevance.

A firm may be highly dependent on nature while this dependency remains poorly measured,
weakly disclosed, or largely invisible to investors and creditors. Conversely, extensive
sustainability disclosure does not necessarily imply high underlying ecological exposure
or high-quality decision-useful information.

The central scientific problem of this research project is therefore not simply whether
biodiversity matters for finance.

It is to understand:

> **How and under what conditions do firms' biodiversity dependencies and impacts become
> measurable, informationally visible and ultimately financially material?**

The project conceptualises this transformation through a proposed
**Nature-to-Finance Materiality Chain**.

---

## 2. The Three-Materiality Problem

The conceptual framework distinguishes three analytically different forms of materiality:

### 1. Ecological Materiality

The firm's actual dependencies on and impacts on biodiversity, ecosystems and ecosystem
services.

### 2. Informational Materiality

The extent to which these dependencies and impacts become observable through metrics,
corporate reporting, sustainability information and external data.

### 3. Financial Materiality

The extent to which nature-related dependencies, impacts, risks and opportunities affect
variables relevant to financial decision-making.

These three forms of materiality should not be assumed to coincide.

This distinction generates the central research tension:

> **An exposure may be ecologically material without being informationally visible, and
> information may be available without being incorporated into financial decisions.**

The research therefore focuses on the frictions separating these stages.

---

## 3. Ecological Materiality

Ecological materiality concerns the relationship between corporate economic activity and
the natural systems on which that activity depends or which it affects.

Relevant dimensions may include:

- ecosystem-service dependencies;
- biodiversity impacts and pressures;
- sectoral dependence on natural capital;
- geographic exposure;
- location-specific ecosystem vulnerability;
- supply-chain and value-chain dependencies;
- physical climate–nature interactions.

A key principle of the framework is that ecological materiality exists independently of
whether a firm reports it.

Consequently:

**Ecological exposure ≠ ESG score ≠ corporate disclosure.**

This distinction is essential for empirical identification.

---

## 4. Informational Materiality

Informational materiality concerns the transformation of ecological exposure into
observable and decision-useful information.

Potential information channels include:

- sustainability reports;
- ESG indicators;
- biodiversity metrics;
- regulatory disclosures;
- governance information;
- geospatial information;
- annual reports;
- corporate textual disclosures;
- NLP-derived indicators;
- AI-assisted information extraction.

The research therefore distinguishes **disclosure quantity** from **disclosure quality**.

Information quality may depend on:

- specificity;
- comparability;
- verifiability;
- temporal consistency;
- location specificity;
- value-chain coverage;
- quantitative metrics;
- governance information;
- targets and monitoring;
- alignment with independently measured ecological exposure.

This creates an important empirical distinction:

> **More disclosure does not necessarily mean better information.**

---

## 5. Financial Materiality

Financial materiality concerns whether nature-related dependencies, impacts, risks or
opportunities affect financially relevant outcomes.

Potential transmission channels include:

- financing conditions;
- cost of debt;
- cost of capital;
- credit risk;
- firm valuation;
- profitability;
- expected cash flows;
- risk premia;
- corporate resilience;
- investor decisions;
- lender decisions.

The project does **not** assume ex ante that every biodiversity exposure is financially
material.

Financial materiality is therefore treated as an **empirical outcome to be tested**, not
as a premise of the research.

---

## 6. Nature-to-Finance Materiality Chain

The proposed conceptual architecture is:

**Ecological Dependencies & Impacts**

↓

### Ecological Materiality

↓

**Measurement Friction**

↓

*Ecological Metrics • ESG Data • Geospatial Data • AI/NLP*

↓

### Informational Materiality

↓

**Disclosure & Regulatory Friction**

↓

*Corporate Reporting • Governance • Double Materiality*

↓

**Financial Transmission Friction**

↓

### Financial Materiality

↓

**Financial Decision-Making**

---

### Core Scientific Sequence

> **Nature → Measurement → Information → Regulation → Finance → Decision**

The objective is to identify where, when and why transmission occurs — or fails to occur —
along this chain.

---

## 7. Transmission Frictions

### 7.1 Measurement Friction

Biodiversity is multidimensional, spatially heterogeneous and difficult to summarise
through a single universal indicator.

Measurement error can therefore create a gap between underlying ecological exposure and
observable ESG or sustainability indicators.

This motivates the comparison of conventional ESG measures with ecological, textual and
location-sensitive information.

### 7.2 Disclosure Friction

Corporate disclosures can differ substantially in quantity, specificity, credibility and
ecological relevance.

Disclosure may be incomplete, selective or poorly aligned with actual ecological
dependencies and impacts.

The project therefore investigates a potential:

**Ecological Exposure–Disclosure Gap**

### 7.3 Regulatory Friction

Reporting regulation can modify firms' incentives and information-production processes.

However:

> **Regulatory compliance ≠ automatically decision-useful information.**

The research therefore distinguishes formal compliance from genuine improvements in
information quality and ecological alignment.

### 7.4 Financial Transmission Friction

Even when nature-related information is available, financial actors may not immediately
or fully incorporate it into their decisions.

Transmission may depend on:

- information quality;
- investor attention;
- lender information;
- nature dependence;
- sector;
- uncertainty;
- regulatory salience;
- investment horizon;
- market learning.

---

## 8. Theoretical Foundations

The project adopts a parsimonious multi-theoretical architecture.

No single theory is assumed to explain the entire Nature-to-Finance transmission process.

### 8.1 Natural Capital and Ecosystem Services

Natural-capital approaches explain why ecosystem degradation may affect production,
operating conditions, resilience and economic value.

They provide the economic foundation for analysing corporate dependencies on nature.

### 8.2 Externalities

Externality theory explains why corporate impacts on nature may not be fully internalised
in private economic decisions or market prices.

This creates a potential divergence between private financial materiality and broader
ecological impact materiality.

### 8.3 Information Asymmetry and Disclosure

Information asymmetry provides a mechanism through which ecological exposure may remain
financially invisible when firms, investors, lenders and other stakeholders possess
different levels or qualities of information.

Disclosure can potentially reduce this information gap.

### 8.4 Stakeholder Theory

Stakeholder theory explains why firms face heterogeneous demands for nature-related
information from investors, regulators, employees, communities and other stakeholders.

### 8.5 Legitimacy Theory

Legitimacy theory provides a basis for distinguishing substantive environmental
information from disclosure motivated predominantly by reputational or legitimacy
considerations.

### 8.6 Institutional Theory

Institutional theory helps explain how regulation, standards and professional norms alter
organisational reporting practices and information production.

---

## 9. Double Materiality

Double materiality is treated here as a **regulatory and reporting principle**, rather
than as an independent academic theory.

It connects two perspectives:

**Impact materiality**

→ how corporate activities affect people and the environment.

**Financial materiality**

→ how sustainability-related risks and opportunities affect the firm.

This distinction provides an institutional bridge between ecological impact and financial
relevance.

---

## 10. Critical Perspective

The project recognises that nature-related financial materiality cannot be reduced to a
purely technical measurement problem.

### Market Efficiency vs. Persistent Externalities

Financial markets may progressively incorporate nature-related information while
ecological externalities remain incompletely reflected in prices.

### Monetisation vs. Plural Values of Nature

Financial valuation may facilitate decision-making without capturing all ecological,
social, cultural and intrinsic values associated with biodiversity.

### Optimisation vs. Precaution

Where ecological thresholds, irreversibility and deep uncertainty are important,
conventional optimisation may be insufficient and precautionary approaches may become
relevant.

These tensions are treated as boundary conditions for interpreting empirical results
rather than as contradictions that must be resolved through a single theoretical model.

---

## 11. Testable Implications

The framework generates five principal empirical propositions.

### P1 — Ecological–Information Gap

Underlying ecological exposure and conventional ESG indicators need not be perfectly
aligned.

### P2 — Disclosure Quality

Greater disclosure quantity does not necessarily imply greater ecological alignment or
greater decision usefulness.

### P3 — Regulatory Transmission

Mandatory sustainability-reporting requirements may alter the quality, specificity and
ecological alignment of corporate biodiversity disclosure.

### P4 — Information–Finance Transmission

The financial consequences of biodiversity exposure may depend on the quality and
observability of nature-related information.

### P5 — Information Integration

Combining ecological, textual, governance and financial information may provide
incremental decision value relative to conventional ESG information alone.

These propositions are deliberately falsifiable.

**Null results are scientifically informative**, because they can identify stages at
which the Nature-to-Finance Materiality Chain weakens or breaks.

---

## 12. Five-Study Research Architecture

| Study | Scientific Function | Core Question |
|---|---|---|
| **A1 — STRUCTURE** | Map the biodiversity–climate–finance field | How has nature emerged as a financial research object? |
| **A2 — MEASURE** | Compare ecological exposure, ESG and textual information | Is ecological materiality adequately represented by available information? |
| **A3 — IDENTIFY** | Analyse sustainability-reporting regulation | Does regulation reduce informational friction? |
| **A4 — TRANSMIT** | Examine financial outcomes | When does ecological exposure become financially material? |
| **A5 — INTEGRATE** | Combine multiple information sources | Does integrated nature-related information improve financial decision-making? |

The five studies constitute a cumulative research design:

> **STRUCTURE → MEASURE → IDENTIFY → TRANSMIT → INTEGRATE**

---

## 13. Scientific Positioning

The **Nature-to-Finance Materiality Chain** is proposed as an integrative conceptual
framework developed within this research project.

It is **not presented as an established theory**.

Its purpose is to organise and empirically investigate the mechanisms through which
ecological dependencies and impacts may become measurable, informationally visible and
financially relevant.

The framework creates a common scientific thread across sustainable finance,
biodiversity economics, corporate disclosure, financial economics, governance and
computational methods.

Its central contribution is therefore not to assume that nature is always financially
material, but to investigate:

> **when, how, through which information mechanisms, and under which institutional
> conditions ecological materiality becomes financial materiality.**
