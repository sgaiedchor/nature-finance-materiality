# Theoretical Foundations

## Purpose

The Nature-to-Finance Materiality project does not seek to construct an artificial
single theory of biodiversity finance.

Instead, it combines complementary theoretical mechanisms to explain different stages
of the transmission from ecological exposure to financial decision-making.

---

## 1. Natural Capital and Ecosystem Services

Natural-capital approaches recognise ecosystems and biodiversity as foundations of
economic production and human welfare.

For firms, ecosystem services can enter production through provisioning, regulating,
supporting and other ecological functions.

The degradation of these services can therefore generate operational, supply-chain,
transition and potentially financial consequences.

### Role in this project

Natural capital primarily explains the transition:

**Nature → Ecological Materiality**

### Key foundation

- Dasgupta, P. (2021). *The Economics of Biodiversity: The Dasgupta Review.*

---

## 2. Externalities

Environmental externalities arise when economic activities impose environmental costs
or benefits that are not fully reflected in private market transactions.

Biodiversity impacts can therefore remain economically important while being only
partially reflected in corporate prices, financial statements or investment decisions.

### Role in this project

Externality theory explains why:

**Ecological Impact ≠ Automatically Financial Materiality**

It also provides a conceptual connection with impact materiality.

---

## 3. Information Asymmetry

Nature-related information is distributed unevenly among firms, managers, investors,
creditors, regulators and other stakeholders.

If ecological dependencies and impacts are difficult to observe, financial actors may
make decisions using incomplete information.

Corporate disclosure and external ecological information can potentially reduce this
information asymmetry.

### Role in this project

Information asymmetry explains the transition:

**Ecological Materiality → Informational Materiality → Financial Decision**

### Key foundation

- Healy, P. M., & Palepu, K. G. (2001). Information asymmetry, corporate
  disclosure, and the capital markets.

---

## 4. Stakeholder Theory

Stakeholder theory recognises that firms interact with multiple groups whose interests
extend beyond shareholders.

Nature-related reporting can therefore respond to information demands from:

- investors;
- creditors;
- regulators;
- employees;
- customers;
- civil society;
- local communities;
- other affected stakeholders.

### Role in this project

Stakeholder theory contributes primarily to understanding corporate incentives to
produce and communicate sustainability information.

### Key foundation

- Freeman, R. E. (1984). *Strategic Management: A Stakeholder Approach.*

---

## 5. Legitimacy Theory

Environmental disclosure can provide substantive information but can also respond to
legitimacy and reputational pressures.

Consequently, the existence or volume of biodiversity-related disclosure cannot by
itself establish information quality.

### Role in this project

Legitimacy theory motivates the distinction:

**Disclosure Quantity ≠ Disclosure Quality ≠ Ecological Alignment**

### Key foundation

- Suchman, M. C. (1995). Managing legitimacy: Strategic and institutional
  approaches.

---

## 6. Institutional Theory

Corporate reporting practices are influenced by regulatory requirements, professional
standards, organisational norms and institutional pressures.

Changes in sustainability-reporting requirements may therefore modify how firms identify,
measure and disclose nature-related information.

### Role in this project

Institutional theory primarily supports:

**Regulation → Information Production → Disclosure**

### Key foundation

- DiMaggio, P. J., & Powell, W. W. (1983). The iron cage revisited:
  Institutional isomorphism and collective rationality in organizational fields.

---

## 7. Double Materiality

Double materiality is not treated as an independent academic theory.

It is treated as a regulatory and reporting framework connecting:

### Impact Materiality
How the undertaking affects people and the environment.

### Financial Materiality
How sustainability-related risks and opportunities may affect the undertaking.

Within the Nature-to-Finance Materiality project, double materiality provides an
institutional bridge between ecological impact and financial relevance.

### Key institutional resource

- [EFRAG Knowledge Hub](https://knowledgehub.efrag.org/)

---

## 8. Nature-Related Risk Frameworks

Nature-related financial-risk frameworks provide operational guidance for identifying
dependencies, impacts, risks and opportunities.

They are used as institutional and measurement resources rather than substitutes for
academic theory.

### Key resources

- [TNFD Recommendations](https://tnfd.global/recommendations/)
- [NGFS](https://www.ngfs.net/)

---

## 9. Theoretical Mapping

| Stage | Principal Lens | Research Function |
|---|---|---|
| Nature → Economy | Natural Capital | Explain ecological dependency |
| Impact → Market Failure | Externalities | Explain incomplete internalisation |
| Exposure → Information | Information Asymmetry | Explain informational friction |
| Disclosure | Stakeholder + Legitimacy | Explain reporting incentives and quality |
| Regulation | Institutional Theory | Explain organisational response |
| Impact ↔ Finance | Double Materiality Framework | Connect impact and financial perspectives |

---

## 10. Integrative Position

The project deliberately avoids theoretical overloading.

The theoretical architecture is modular:

> **Natural Capital explains why nature matters economically.**

> **Externalities explain why ecological impacts may remain outside private prices.**

> **Information Asymmetry explains why ecological exposure may remain invisible.**

> **Stakeholder and Legitimacy theories explain disclosure incentives and limitations.**

> **Institutional Theory explains regulatory responses.**

> **Double Materiality provides the contemporary reporting bridge between impacts and
> financial effects.**

Together, these mechanisms support the empirical investigation of the proposed
Nature-to-Finance Materiality Chain without presenting that framework as an established
theory.
